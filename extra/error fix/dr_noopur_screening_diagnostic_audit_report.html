Overall verdict: Do not publish unchanged.

The page is visually organised and covers screening methods, age bands, FAQs and a consultation CTA. However, the age-based screening schedule and several diagnostic statements are presented as fixed rules. They need review and approval by the treating breast specialist and hospital team. The “greater than 99% diagnostic accuracy” statement and the footer promise about complete tumour clearance are especially high-risk wording.

1. High-priority clinical and trust claims

Critical – clinician review

1. “Greater than 99% diagnostic accuracy”

Location: Page 1, “How We Evaluate a Breast Symptom”.

Concern: A numerical accuracy claim is highly specific, but the page does not identify the source, population, test combination, endpoint or conditions behind the figure. Triple assessment is described as clinical examination, imaging and biopsy when indicated; the text should not imply a universal accuracy rate.

Action: Remove the percentage unless the doctor can provide a reliable, applicable source and approve the exact context. Safer editorial direction: “A structured assessment combines clinical examination, appropriate imaging and tissue sampling when indicated to help reach an accurate diagnosis.”

Critical – clinician review

2. Age-based screening schedule is written as a universal protocol

Location: Page 2, cards for Age 20–39, Age 40–49 and Age 50 & Above.

Concern: The cards prescribe monthly self-examination, clinical examination every 1–3 years, annual/biennial mammography with ultrasound, and annual mammography from age 50. Screening decisions can depend on personal risk, family history, prior findings, breast density, local guidance and the clinician’s assessment. The document does not cite the guideline or explain the intended population.

Action: Do not present these age bands as a universal recommendation until the doctor confirms the exact guideline, country/population, starting age, interval and exceptions. Add a visible note that screening is individualised and high-risk patients may need a different plan.

Critical – source needed

3. “Early detection … yields survival and cure rates exceeding 90%”

Location: Page 2, Age 50 & Above card.

Concern: This outcome figure is not sourced and may be interpreted as a guaranteed result. Survival varies by cancer type, stage, biology, treatment and population. “Cure rates” is also imprecise in this context.

Action: Remove the percentage unless it is tied to a named, current and relevant source with context. Consider a non-numeric statement such as “Screening can help detect some breast cancers earlier, when treatment may be more effective.” Have the clinician approve the wording.

Critical – rewrite

4. Footer repeats an absolute oncologic and aesthetic outcome promise

Location: Page 2 footer.

Current wording visible: “Combining complete oncologic tumor clearance with oncoplastic aesthetic preservation for every woman.”

Concern: “Complete … clearance” and “for every woman” can sound like a guaranteed cancer and cosmetic outcome. Neither should be promised in general website copy.

Suggested direction: “Treatment planning aims to follow appropriate oncologic principles while considering breast shape and reconstruction options when clinically suitable.”

High – clinician review

5. “Biopsy does not cause cancer to spread or grow faster” and “no cancer treatment should ever begin without histological confirmation”

Location: Page 2, FAQ “Does a biopsy cause cancer to spread?”.

Concern: The first sentence is categorical and the second uses “ever”, another absolute. The page should explain the usual clinical rationale without implying there are no exceptions or unusual circumstances.

Action: Ask the doctor to approve a precise FAQ response, including the distinction between routine diagnostic biopsy and rare clinical exceptions. Avoid “ever” and other blanket terms unless the clinician confirms them.

High – clinician review

6. Mammography timing and breast ultrasound claims need qualification

Location: Page 2, FAQs “Does a mammogram hurt?” and “Why do I need both Ultrasound and Mammography?”.

Concern: “Schedule your exam a week after your menstrual cycle” is stated as a general instruction. The “perfect complementary scan” wording is promotional, and the explanation may imply both tests are routinely required for all women with dense breasts.

Action: Have the specialist confirm whether cycle timing is appropriate as general advice and clarify that the choice of mammography, ultrasound or both depends on the clinical indication and individual assessment.

2. Repetition and page structure

Medium

7. Diagnostic workflow is repeated across the hero and evaluation panel

Location: Page 1 hero and “How We Evaluate a Breast Symptom”.

Observation: The hero already lists digital mammography, 3D breast ultrasound and image-guided biopsy. The next panel repeats the diagnostic pathway and the three assessment components.

Recommendation: Keep the hero as a short summary and use the evaluation panel to explain the actual sequence: clinical assessment, appropriate imaging, then sampling if indicated. Avoid repeating the same modality list twice.

Medium

8. “Digital Mammography” appears as both a hero image label and a diagnostic card

Location: Page 1 hero image caption and “Key Diagnostic Investigations”.

Recommendation: The repeat is not necessarily wrong, but use the hero caption as a visual label only. The diagnostic card should carry the useful explanation and the indication, avoiding duplicated descriptive copy.

6. Final audit conclusion

What works: The page has a logical sequence from screening overview to diagnostic investigations, screening timeline, FAQs and appointment CTA. The visual hierarchy is easy to follow.

Fix before publishing: First, send the screening age bands, intervals, accuracy percentage, outcome statistic and biopsy FAQ to the breast specialist for explicit approval. Second, remove the absolute footer promise. Third, consolidate repeated diagnostic descriptions and verify the disclaimer and registration details.
