Overall verdict: Needs clinical and editorial revision before publishing.

The page has a useful education-library structure: featured breast-cancer guide, six learning topics, a downloadable self-examination guide, FAQs and a consultation CTA. However, the featured guide contains a strong survival statistic, the self-examination content is promoted as a monthly protocol, and the footer repeats an absolute surgical-outcome promise. Medical content should be reviewed and approved by the named specialist, with sources linked directly to the relevant claims.

1. High-priority medical and evidence claims

Critical – source and clinician approval

1. “Early-Stage Cure Rate: Detected at Stage 1, long-term survival exceeds 90%...”

Location: Page 1, featured clinical guide “Understanding Breast Cancer: Key Facts & Early Detection”.

Concern: This is a numerical outcome claim without a visible source, date, population, cancer subtype or definition of “long-term survival”. Survival and cure are not interchangeable terms. The claim may be interpreted as a guaranteed outcome for an individual patient.

Action: Either remove the percentage or cite a reliable, current source and specify exactly what the statistic measures, the population and the time period. Have the breast specialist approve the final wording. Avoid calling survival data a “cure rate”.

Critical – clinician review

2. Monthly breast self-examination is framed as a standard screening protocol

Location: Page 1 learning-library card “Breast Self-Examination: Step-by-Step”; Page 2 downloadable “Breast Self-Examination Protocol”; Page 2 FAQ on best time of month.

Concern: The same monthly self-examination instruction appears in several places and is presented as a protocol. The page should distinguish breast awareness / familiarity with normal breast changes from a formal screening test. The clinical appropriateness and wording of a monthly self-examination recommendation need specialist approval.

Action: Ask the doctor to confirm whether the resource should be framed as “breast awareness” or as a specific self-examination protocol. Make clear that self-checking does not replace recommended clinical assessment or imaging, and that a new symptom should be assessed rather than waiting for the next monthly check.

Critical – clinician review

3. “7 to 10 days after the first day of your menstrual period” instruction

Location: Page 2, FAQ “When is the best time of the month to perform a breast self-exam?”.

Concern: The advice is stated as a universal instruction for menstruating women, with a separate brief note for post-menopausal women. The page does not explain the clinical source or how to adapt the guidance for irregular cycles, hormonal contraception, pregnancy or other circumstances.

Action: Have the specialist approve the exact timing guidance and its scope. Consider simpler, inclusive wording that focuses on becoming familiar with one’s usual breast appearance and feel, if that is the intended educational objective.

High – source needed

4. “Only approximately 5% to 10% of breast cancers are linked to inherited genetic mutations...”

Location: Page 2, FAQ “Is breast cancer hereditary?”.

Concern: The percentage is presented without a source or explanation of what “hereditary” means. The second part says the vast majority occur “spontaneously without a strong hereditary link”; that may oversimplify the role of family history and other risk factors.

Action: Provide a current source and have the doctor confirm the percentage and phrasing. Explain that family history can still matter even when no inherited mutation is known; do not imply that absence of a known mutation means absence of increased risk.

High – evidence/source review

5. “Early-stage breast cancer frequently does not cause pain or obvious lumps”

Location: Page 1 featured guide introduction.

Concern: The intended message is that breast cancer may not cause obvious symptoms early. But the wording could be misread as reassurance that absence of pain or a lump means there is no concern.

Action: Rephrase to say that early breast cancer may have no noticeable symptoms, while any new breast change should be assessed by a qualified clinician. Have the doctor approve the final wording.

2. Repeated content and structure

Medium

6. Self-examination topic is repeated in three separate content blocks

Location: Page 1 learning-library card; Page 2 downloadable protocol; Page 2 FAQ.

Problem: The step-by-step article, downloadable protocol and FAQ all address monthly self-examination and timing. These can work together, but currently they risk repeating the same instruction without clarifying what each resource adds.

Recommendation: Use the library card for a short introduction, the download for the detailed illustrated instructions, and the FAQ for a brief timing clarification plus a link to the full guide. Keep terminology consistent throughout.

Medium

7. Early detection and screening message overlaps with the Screening page

Location: Page 1 featured guide and Page 2 self-examination CTA/FAQ. The previously supplied Screening & Diagnostic Assessment page also contains age-based screening, self-examination and early-detection messaging.

Problem: Across the website, the same topics are spread across the Screening page and Education page. This is not automatically wrong, but repeated advice can drift into inconsistent wording or different instructions.

Recommendation: Make the Screening page the source for screening and diagnostic pathways. Make Education the source for plain-language explainers and downloadable resources. Cross-link rather than reproducing different versions of the same protocol. Keep all medical recommendations aligned after clinician approval.

Medium

8. Featured guide and learning-library cards need clearer source ownership

Location: Page 1 hero and six learning-library cards.

Problem: The hero says “doctor-verified clinical information”, but individual cards do not visibly show author, reviewer, last-reviewed date or references. A blanket statement at the top is less useful than evidence attached to each resource.

Recommendation: Add “Written by / Medically reviewed by”, reviewer designation and “Last reviewed” date to each article page. Add references at the end of medical articles and identify the exact guideline/version where applicable.

Must rewrite

9. Footer repeats absolute outcome promise

Location: Page 2 footer.

Current wording visible: “Combining complete oncologic tumor clearance with oncoplastic aesthetic preservation for every woman.”

Concern: This sounds like a guarantee of cancer clearance and aesthetic preservation for every patient.

Suggested direction: “Treatment planning aims to follow appropriate oncologic principles while considering breast shape and reconstruction options when clinically suitable.”

6. Final audit conclusion

What works: The education page has a strong, easy-to-follow layout and useful content categories: breast cancer basics, self-care, biopsy understanding, surgery preparation, lymphoedema rehabilitation and nutrition/lifestyle support. The downloadable resource and FAQs give visitors clear next steps.

Fix first: Remove or substantiate the survival statistic; have the doctor review self-examination instructions and hereditary-risk wording; attach medical review dates and references to each guide; remove the absolute footer promise; and align overlapping advice with the Screening page.

Scope limitation: This is an editorial audit of the visible content in the supplied 3-page PDF. It does not independently validate clinical claims, screening recommendations, statistics, guideline compliance or medical accuracy. Any clinical statement should be checked by the treating specialist and supported by appropriate sources before publication.
